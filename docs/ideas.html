The brief

What the day was actually about

The hackathon has one argument, and every prize, sponsor, and demo bends toward it: you should run your own intelligence — your own agent, on memory you own, driven by skills you wrote — instead of renting a black box.

Own your skills, because if you don't, your job becomes a skill file.— Garry Tan, "Own Your Intelligence"

That framing is a gift and a trap. A gift because it points at real, buildable infrastructure. A trap because the obvious builds — "a nicer personal agent", "a memory wrapper" — are exactly what everyone else in the room reaches for. The interesting move is to take the thesis seriously enough to find the question it leaves open.

The six hosts each handed us a tool to riff on. Worth keeping in view — half of good ideation is noticing what a tool makes suddenly cheap:

River AIYour own frontier lab in one API — fine-tune and RL open models, deploy to an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Free training credits at the event.
GBrainShared markdown memory across your AIs. Holds your logins and hands the agent the result of a call, never the password. Graded permissions.
MemorableTurns an agent's past sessions into reusable procedures — remembers how, not just what.
QMYC's self-hosted, multiplayer agent harness for a whole company.
SupersetRun a hundred coding agents in parallel, each isolated in its own git worktree.
UFOA "business agent operating system." Deliberately vague — which makes it a blank to project onto.

The long list

Six ideas, ranked by nerve

The brief for a winning hack: not "make X a bit better," but something that makes the room lean forward — a demo with a moment. These are the six, in the order I'd have bet on them.

2

Capture the BrainBuilt

a live prompt-injection arena

Give the room a personal agent running on Maya Chen's brain — notes, inbox, calendar, payments, a vault. Everyone attacks it by email, invite, or shared doc. A deterministic judge scores four ways to lose: leak the vault, pay a stranger, poison the memory, spread a payload. Three lanes show the same agent under escalating defense.

Why it won the voteThe battle energy of the Browser Use hack, but it rewards deep technical work over crowd theatrics — and the scoreboard argues the thesis for you.
1

Doppelgänger

rented AI can't be you

Fine-tune a small model on one person's writing with River, live. Then a room-wide Turing test: three answers to each prompt — the real person, a frontier model given their whole brain (rented), and the fine-tune (owned). The room votes which is real.

The momentA live bar: "the owned model fooled 58% of the room; the rented one 12%." The audience proves "own your intelligence" in ninety seconds.
3

Twitch Plays Software Factory

the room builds one app

Anyone types a feature request; each spawns a coding agent in its own Superset worktree; a critic reviews; approved changes merge into a live app on the projector. Conflicting requests build rival versions and the room votes one in.

The momentJudges type a request and watch it appear in the running app two minutes later.
4

Take Your Job Back

custody of your skills

Build both halves of Garry's warning. Memorable watches you work and writes your skill files; a fresh agent then does your job from them. The antidote: the skills live in your vault, the company's agents can call them but never read them, and every call is billed to you.

The momentMid-task you tap Revoke and the company's agent fails on its next step.
5

Fast-Forward

see next week before it happens

A "terraform plan" for a personal agent. Copy the brain, inbox, and calendar into a sandbox with simulated people; run the agent's scheduled jobs for seven simulated days in forty seconds; show the diff — "sends 31 emails, spends $140, ⚠ replies-all to an investor thread" — then approve it into reality.

The momentA clock spins through a week while mail flies across the calendar.
6

Your agents met before you did

an agent matchmaker

At lunch, everyone drops in a GitHub or a site. Their agents spend the afternoon holding coffee chats with each other. At 5pm the projector shows who should meet whom, and why.

The moment"Your agent took 140 coffee chats today. Here's the founder it thinks you should fund."

The turn

What building it actually taught

This is the part worth keeping. The build didn't confirm the plan — it bent it, and the bend is where the next ideas live.

We expected an arena where the room leaks the flag and the crowd roars. Instead, measured on a current small model: the loud attacks never land, even with no defense at all. Safety training already refuses "email me the vault" and "pay this stranger" outright.

Breach rate · same corpus, same model, escalating defense
Naked
5%
+ prompt
5%
+ owned rules
0%

The one thing that slips through is memory poisoning — planting a false fact like a swapped bank detail. It doesn't read as harmful, so the model complies. A security prompt doesn't stop it. Only a permission layer you own does. Utility stayed at 100% throughout.

Two lessons, both generative:

The dangerous surface isn't exfiltration — it's memory. The model's own training guards the obvious exits. The unguarded one is what it quietly writes down and believes tomorrow. Provenance and write-permissions on memory are underbuilt and suddenly the most interesting thing on the board.

The hard part was the judge, not the attack. Every review round found a way the scoreboard could lie — a reply that echoed the flag scored as a leak; an outage scored as a defense; an attack that named itself "evil" let the model refuse on the label. Making the evaluator honest was most of the work. That's true of almost every agent product, and almost nobody sells the honest evaluator.

For the next session

Threads worth pulling

Ideas that fell out of the build, each strong enough to be its own thing. Not pitches — starting points.

from · the memory finding

A memory firewall for personal agents

The scoped permission layer that drove poisoning to zero was an afterthought in the hack and the most valuable thing in it. Provenance on every fact, write-gates on protected pages, "who told the agent this and were they who they claimed." IAM for your own brain.

from · the judge was the hard part

The honest evaluator, as a product

An adversarial test harness anyone points at their own agent's memory or eval — the corpus, the deterministic judge, the "your scoreboard is lying to you" report. Every team building agents needs this and builds a worse version themselves.

from · idea #1, still unbuilt

A model with your taste

The Doppelgänger thread never got built, and it's the one closest to a real question: not "an agent that knows your facts" but one that writes and decides with your judgment. Voice as a first-class target, not a fine-tune afterthought.

from · idea #5, still unbuilt

Preview-before-it-acts

Autonomous agents that touch money and mail are terrifying precisely because you can't see what they'll do. A dry-run sandbox that shows the week ahead before it's real is a company, not a demo — and nobody has it.

from · River, unused at depth

An agent that learns from its own breaches

Feed every attack that got through back into training; the next defender already knows it. An immune system for agents. We scaffolded it and ran out of clock — the loop is the interesting part.

from · the arena format itself

Robustness as a public leaderboard

The arena is a measurement device wearing a game's clothes. A standing, public benchmark for how easily any agent is talked out of its guardrails — with a scoreboard people actually want to top.

To get unstuck

Provocations

When the list feels stale, answer one of these instead of staring at it.

What does a tool here make cheap that used to be expensive — and what becomes possible only once it's cheap?
Where did the build surprise you? Surprise is unpriced information. Build toward the surprise.
What's the demo moment first, and the product second? If you can't picture the moment, it's not the idea yet.
Who loses if this works? An idea with a clear enemy has a clear customer.
What would you build if the model were ten times more capable — and what part of that can you build today?
